Why a decrease then an increase does not return you to the start

Take 100, cut it by 20% to get 80, then add 20% back: 80 × 1.2 = 96, not 100. The second percentage is applied to a smaller base. To reverse a 20% decrease you need a 25% increase. This is the single most common percentage mistake, and it appears constantly in discount and salary-cut arithmetic.

Percentage points versus percent

If a rate moves from 5% to 7%, that is a rise of two percentage points but a 40% increase in the rate itself. Both statements are true and they describe the same change — mixing them up is a common source of misleading claims in reporting.

Percent change when the starting value is negative

The formula divides by the absolute value of A, so a move from −50 to −25 registers as a 50% improvement rather than a −50% one. Percent change on quantities that cross zero is ambiguous by nature and is usually better reported as an absolute difference.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate a percentage increase?

Subtract the old value from the new one, divide by the old value, then multiply by 100. From 200 to 250: (250 − 200) ÷ 200 × 100 = 25%.

Why doesn't reversing a percentage get me back to the original?

Because the second percentage applies to a different base. A 20% fall needs a 25% rise to undo it, since the rise is calculated on the reduced figure.

What is the difference between percent and percentage points?

A move from 5% to 7% is two percentage points, but a 40% increase in the rate. Percentage points measure the gap between two percentages; percent measures relative change.

How do I work out the original price before a discount?

Use the reverse mode. If an item costs 80 after a 20% discount, it was 80 ÷ 0.80 = 100 originally.

Can percent change be more than 100%?

Yes for increases — doubling is +100%, tripling is +200%. Decreases cannot go below −100%, because that would mean the value fell past zero.
